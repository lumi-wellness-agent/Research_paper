## [ABSTRACT] A B S T R A C T

Building on a body of research examining the influence of natural versus urban images on attention, the purpose of the present study was to examine attention restoration with (1) a large sample size, (2) a broader image set that was more representative of typical natural and urban environments, and (3) an increased number of task trials to increase the likelihood of more thorough attentional depletion. In order to compare the restorative effects of natural versus urban images on attention, participants completed the Sustained Attention to Response Task (SART; a measure of sustained attention) before and after they viewed either natural or urban images. When compared to the urban condition, participants in the nature condition did not demonstrate improved perfor­ mance in the post-image exposure SART. Bayesian analyses also indicated support for the null hypothesis. Thus, this study provides evidence that is inconsistent with the consensus view that images of natural settings restore attention more than images of urban settings.

## [INTRODUCTION] 1. Introduction

In environmental psychology, there is vast interest in understanding how natural environments may more effectively restore depleted attentional capacities - and thus lead to improved post-exposure attentional performance - than urban environments (Berman, Jonides, & Kaplan, 2008; Berto, 2005; Kaplan & Kaplan, 1989; for a review, see; Ohly et al., 2016, or; Stevenson, Schilab, & Bentsen, 2018). To explain the potential restorative benefits of natural environments, Kaplan and colleagues (Kaplan, 1995; Kaplan & Kaplan, 1989) proposed the Attention Restoration Theory (ART), which posits that there are two distinct types of attention: 'involuntary attention', which is uncontrolled and effortless; and 'directed attention', which is limited in capacity, controlled, effortful and can be depleted. According to ART, natural environments hold more restorative potential than urban environments because they are more likely to gently, and involuntarily, guide the viewer's attention in such a way that directed attention is not required and has a chance to rest (e.g., a calmly flowing river; this situation is referred to as "soft fascination", Kaplan, 1995).
<!-- text p.1 -->

While much of the past research has examined the influence of realworld environments on attention, virtual image/video exposures have also been studied, perhaps because of the restorative potential they hold when barriers to real environments (e.g., accessibility, time, financial, and other socioeconomic factors) are present. Pioneering the efforts to test ART in a novel and virtual behavioural paradigm, Berto (2005) provided foundational support for the idea that attention performance can be restored following exposure to restorative images. In this important study, 32 participants completed two rounds of the Sustained Attention to Response Task (SART; Roberston, Manly, Andrade, Bad­ deley, & Yiend, 1997), separated by a the presentation of either 'restorative' images (all natural environments, n = 16), or 'non-­ restorative' images (all urban environments, n = 16). Images with high ratings on the Perceived Restorativeness Scale (PRS) - Short (Berto, 2001; Korpela & Hartig, 1996; Peron, Berto, & Purcell, 2002) were selected for the restorative category, while images with low ratings were selected for the non-restorative category. In the SART, the digits from 1 to 9 are presented one at a time at a constant rate on the computer screen. Participants are required to make key-press responses to non-target stimuli (e.g., 1, 2, 4-9) and withhold the key-press response to a target stimulus (e.g., 3). Berto (2005) found that, compared to those who viewed the lower perceived restoration images, participants who viewed higher perceived restoration images made significantly more correct withholds on target trials and had significantly faster response times during the post-image-exposure SART in comparison to the pre-image-exposure SART. Based on these results, Berto (2005) concluded that the restorative image set was indeed restorative, as indexed by performance on an attention task, while the non-restorative image set was not. Importantly, Berto (2005) also noted that the present research "underscore(s) ART's premise that natural environments are more restorative than urban environments", (Berto, 2005, p. 258).

In the present investigation, our goal was to more directly test the conclusion that natural environments restore attention more effectively than do urban environments. As such, the present study is a conceptual replication focusing on one conclusion drawn from Berto's work. We chose to focus on this conclusion because Berto's (2005) findings are commonly cited as showing that nature environments confer greater attentional restoration than urban environments (e.g., Abraham, Som­ merhalder, & Abel, 2010; Atchley, Strayer, & Atchley, 2012; Berman et al., 2008; Bratman, Daily, Levy, & Gross, 2015; Bratman, Hamilton, & Daily, 2012; Carrus, Passiatore, Pirchio, & Scopelliti, 2015; Kaplan & Berman, 2010; Sonnentag, Venz, & Casper, 2017; van den Berg, Maas, Verheig, & Groenewgen, 2010). Importantly, this common interpreta­ tion of Berto's (2005) findings might not be necessitated by the data presented by Berto (2005) because the images used in Berto's study were selected based on predicted restorativeness (high vs. low), and not exclusively on environment type (nature vs. urban; though this distinction was also present within the stimulus set). We hasten to add however, that Berto (2005) might have been primarily interested in the influence of 'restorativeness' on attention, and so our study is not a direct repli­ cation of Berto's work.
<!-- text p.2 -->

Studies that have followed up on Berto's (2005) findings have several critical limitations. First, several attempted replications have continued to use stimuli confounded by restorativeness (e.g., Craig, 2016; Craig & Klein, 2012; Craig, Klein, Menon, & Rinaldo, 2015; Dumke, 2014; Nguyen, Nelson, & Klein, 2018). Second, in several studies, researchers used only one stimulus in each of the urban and nature conditions (e.g., Lee, Williams, Sargent, Williams, & Johnson, 2015; Sanguinetti, 2009; Valtchanov, 2010), thus substantively limiting their generalizability. Finally, we note that the extant replications of Berto's (2005) findings have yielded mixed results (e.g., Craig, 2016; Dumke, 2014; Nguyen, Nelson, & Klein, 2018; Sanguinetti, 2009). The inconsistency in the re­ sults across these studies may be in part due to the underwhelming sample sizes used: 16 participants per group in Berto's study and 25 participants or less per group in a number of publications (e.g., Craig, 2016; Craig et al., 2015; Craig & Klein, 2012; Dumke, 2014; Nguyen, Nelson, & Klein, 2018; Sanguinetti, 2009; Valtchanov, 2010). Thus, it is clear that what is necessary is a well-designed and well-powered con­ ceptual replication of the commonly cited conclusion from Berto (2005), namely that images of natural environments are more restorative than urban environments.

## [INTRODUCTION] 1.1. The present study

Given the abovementioned concerns, the purpose of the current study is threefold. First, we aimed to more directly test the conclusion drawn by Berto (2005) as it is commonly cited in the literature-that exposure to natural environments is more attentionally restorative than exposure to urban environments. To gauge this, we collected partici­ pants' ratings of how typical various images were of natural and urban settings, rather than on the basis of restorativeness. Second, we aimed to test Berto's (2005) conclusion with a larger sample that would be suf­ ficient to detect a small effect size. We determined that 80 participants per group (160 participants total) would be required to detect a fairly small effect size (d = 0.30), with a power of .80 and a two-tailed alpha value of 0.05. Finally, in our study, we increased the number of trials in an attempt to more thoroughly deplete attention during the pre-exposure SART, and to increase the likelihood we would observe any potential attention restoration during the post-exposure SART. Since sustained attention performance declines as a function of time-on-task (a.k.a., the 'vigilance decrement'; Head, Russell, Dorahy, Neumann, &
<!-- text p.2 -->

Helton, 2012; Mackworth, 1948), we predicted that attention depletion would be the greatest at the end of the pre-exposure SART trials, and that attention restoration would be the strongest at the beginning of the post-exposure SART trials.

## [METHODS] 2.1. Participants

Two hundred students (mean age = 19.74, SD = 1.87) from the University of Waterloo participated in this study in exchange for partial course credit. Two participants did not complete the experiment due to technical difficulties and thus could not be included in the analyses. Thirteen students were excluded from the analysis as they were tested during preliminary sessions of the experiment used to train the research assistant, and five participants were excluded due to deviations in the experimental protocol (e.g., the presence of visual/auditory distractions due to other participants; participants completing the SART in other experiments just prior to the present experiment). 1 Thus, a total of 180 participants were included in the analysis (123 female, 54 male, 2 un­ known). Slight overshooting of our planned sample was due to partici­ pants being run in groups and the anticipation of 'no-shows'. Participants were randomly assigned to either the nature or the urban condition and were run in group sizes ranging from 1 to 4 participants.

## [METHODS] 2.3.1. Image selection

One hundred images were selected from various free desktop back­ ground websites. Fifty images contained mostly natural settings and 50 images contained mostly urban settings. The goal was to sample a wide selection of images that varied on a spectrum from natural to urban environments. No settings contained people. Image selection was based on the types of environments that Berto (2005) included in the original picture set: lakes, rivers, seas, hills, woods, orchards, forests, city riv­ ersides, city streets, industrial zones, housing, porches, urban areas, and skyscrapers.
<!-- text p.2 -->

Prior to the commencement of the experiment proper, a pre-scaling study was conducted to determine which subset of images from the initial set of 100 images would be included in the experiment proper. In this pre-scaling study, 100 participants were recruited from the Amazon Mechanical Turk (www.mturk.com). These participants were compen­ sated $3.50 USD for a 30-min study. Participants viewed each image for 6 s. After viewing each image, participants were asked to respond to two slider scales. The first slider scale read: "That is a place that matches my idea of a typical natural environment", and the second slider read: "That is a place that matches my idea of a typical urban environment"; both sliders were anchored by, "(0) Strongly Disagree" and "(100) Strongly Agree".
<!-- text p.2 -->

The ratings of the images from the pre-scaling study were then used to select which images would make up the stimuli sets for the urban and natural conditions in the present study. The ratings for each image was averaged across participants. Difference scores were calculated for each image by subtracting the average urban typicality values from the average nature typicality values. Then, the twenty-five images with the largest positive difference scores were selected as the images for the nature condition, and the twenty-five images with the largest negative difference scores were selected as the images for the urban condition. Once the images were selected, they were balanced on contrast and luminance using Matlab programming (McCrackin & Itier, 2018; Wil­ lenbockel et al., 2010).

## [METHODS] 2.3.2. Sustained attention to Response Task (SART)

The SART (Robertson, Manly, Andrade, Baddeley, & Yiend, 1997) is a computer task in which participants are presented a series of digits (1-9) one at a time and must actively monitor for infrequent targets. Participants were instructed to press the space bar for each "go" non-target digit (1, 2, 4-9), and withhold responses to the "no-go" target digit, (3). For each trial, a single digit from 1 to 9 was randomly selected without replacement to appear for 250 ms in the center of the screen, followed by a "+" mask presented for 900 ms, resulting in a total trial duration of 1150 ms. The critical no-go target digit was presented pseudo-randomly over the duration of the task. The SART was comprised of 70 blocks of nine trials each, for a total of 630 trials, resulting in a total task length of 12 min and 4.5 s. All digits were pre­ sented in white on a black background, using the Courier New typeface in one of five randomly selected sizes: 120, 100, 94, 72, and 48 points.
<!-- text p.3 -->

The primary measure of interest obtained from the SART was com­ mission errors, defined as instances when participants failed to withhold a response to a no-go trial and an important index of failures in sustained attention (Robertson et al., 1997). Secondary measures of attention lapses included go-trial reaction times (RTs), defined as the mean response latencies for all of the go-trials in which a response was made; and omission errors, defined as instances when participants failed to respond to a go-trial (Cheyne, Solman, Carriere, & Smilek, 2009; Rob­ ertson et al., 1997).
<!-- text p.3 -->

## [METHODS] 2.3.3. Image viewing task

Fifty images (25 per condition) selected from the pre-scaling study were presented in colour with dimensions of 800x800px in the center of the screen. The rest of the screen surrounding the images was black. Each image was presented for 15 s, resulting in a total image-viewing period of 6 min and 15 s.

## [RESULTS] 3.1. Data exclusion and analysis overview

Data was first analyzed for violations of normality. Initial descriptive statistics revealed that, while skew and kurtosis values were normal for response times and commission errors, the outputs for omission errors were not within the acceptable skew and kurtosis range (|skew| < 3.0 and |kurtosis| < 10, as per the recommendations of Kline, 1998). This was due to a few outliers in the high end of the omission distribution. To correct for this, participants with an average omission error rate of 3 standard deviations or greater from the mean was excluded listwise from each condition pre- and post-image exposure (as per of the protocol of Seli, Jonker, Solman, Cheyne, & Smilek, 2013). This was done as higher omission rates may be indicative of participants who failed to under­ stand the instructions or willfully decided not to follow instructions. After removing those participants, the skew and kurtosis values for the omissions of each group fell within the normal range (Kline, 1998). The final sample consisted of 171 participants, with 84 participants in the nature condition, and 85 participants in the urban condition.
<!-- text p.3 -->

The data were analyzed in two ways. The first analysis examined each of the SART dependent variables (commission errors, RTs, and omission errors) based on data from all trials of the pre- and postexposure SARTs. The second analysis included only the last 243 trials of the pre-exposure SART and the first 243 trials of the post-exposure SART, in an attempt to more directly mimic Berto's (2005) analysis. In addition, analyses are reported with omission outliers both removed and included.
<!-- text p.3 -->

To determine whether the data provided evidence for the null hy­ pothesis (i.e., that image type does not influence SART performance), we conducted a Bayes analysis. The Bayes factor allowed us to assess the evidence in favour of the null hypothesis (H0) and the alternative hy­ pothesis (H1) given a distribution for the prior probability of these hy­ potheses. A BF01 is the ratio of the likelihood of the data under the null hypothesis compared to the alternative hypothesis. We interpreted the Bayes factors based on the recommendations of Jeffreys (1961).
<!-- text p.3 -->

## [DISCUSSION] 4. Discussion

In a well-powered study, we found no evidence for the claim that post-exposure attention performance was better following exposure to natural, rather than, urban images. In fact, Bayesian analyses suggested strong evidence for the null hypotheses. There are several possible reasons why the present study did not find the same pattern reported by Berto (2005). First, our approach differed from Berto's such that our stimuli were selected based on ratings of typicality rather than restorative po­ tential (to avoid confounding environment type with predicted restor­ ativeness level). Second, each iteration of the SART in our study consisted of 630 trials, which is substantially more the 240 trials included in Berto's (2005) original study. However, this concern is alleviated by the fact that we did not find any difference in performance across conditions when only the first 243 trials of the post-exposure SART were compared to the last 243 trials of the pre-exposure SART (matching Berto's analysis). Finally, we should consider the possibility that the results reported by Berto (2005) might be unreliable owing to the relatively small sample used in the study.
<!-- text p.5 -->

It is important to note that while we failed to find an influence of environment (nature vs. urban) on performance in the SART following virtual exposure, this does not mean that null findings will also occur in well-powered examinations using other types of attention tasks or other methods of nature exposure (i.e., real-world exposure). Stevenson, Schilhab, and Bentsen (2018) reviewed several studies with tasks that may hold greater potential for capturing subtle improvements in per­ formance following natural vs. urban exposure (i.e., the Stroop Task and the Trail Making Task - version B); however, the authors noted that many of these studies were underpowered, had only small to moderate effect sizes, and demonstrated no attentional improvements when the groups within the studies started from equivalent baseline measures (Stevenson et al., 2018). With the publication of this replication, we hope to encourage further well-powered replications to provide more decisive evidence regarding the possibility of attention restoration following nature exposure across attention tasks and experimental contexts.

Impor­ tantly, all interactions remained null when exclusions were included in the full SART analysis and Bayes factors also provided evidence against the inclusion of an interaction model of the data.
