## [ABSTRACT] A B S T R A C T

Much prior research suggests that exposure to natural environments is associated with improved cognitive performance, in comparison to non-natural exposures. However, the presence and size of such nature benefits are inconsistent. This meta-analysis aimed to determine the influence of nature exposure duration, a likely moder­ ating factor, on cognitive performance change. In total, 273 individual outcomes from 80 studies were included in correlated hierarchical effects (CHE) multilevel meta-analyses. When analysing all cognitive outcomes, the average performance improvement was larger for nature exposures compared to non-natural comparators. Of 11 cognitive domains identified in the literature, Working Memory and Attentional Control were the only domains to show reliable nature benefits. However, subgroup analysis suggested that effect size differences between cognitive domains may not be significant. Meta-regressions indicated exposure duration significantly moderated natural/non-natural differences in restoration in a non-linear relationship, though the shape and significance of this relationship within each cognitive domain was not consistent. Nature benefits were generally larger for participants who underwent cognitive fatigue, but other experimental design variations did not produce meaningful differences in effects. Overall, effects sizes were small and heterogeneity was substantial. The largest difference in cognitive restoration between natural/non-natural exposures appears to occur after approximately 30 min of environmental exposure.

## [INTRODUCTION] 1.1. Background

The ability to remain focused on specific tasks for extended periods is supported by selective or directed attention, a cognitive resource described by Attention Restoration Theory (ART; Kaplan, 1995) as the conscious, volitional focus on salient stimuli and the simultaneous engagement of inhibitory control processes that supress distracting stimuli.
<!-- text p.1 -->

Continued application of directed attention can result in attentional fatigue and consequent declines in performance on tasks that are dependent on this cognitive resource. The central claim of ART is that natural environments produce greater opportunity for recovery from attentional fatigue. This is largely attributed to natural environments

The result being improved attention restoration in nature, relative to non-natural settings such as built/urban or indoor environments.

Extensive research has investigated the effect of environmental ex­ posures on cognition. While some studies have reported strong benefits of nature exposures on attentional outcomes relative to non-natural exposures (Berman et al., 2008; Berto, 2005), null differences are also commonly reported (Hicks et al., 2020; Johnson et al., 2021). An early systematic review and meta-analysis by (Bowler et al., 2010) concluded there was a moderate effect favouring natural settings, however, given only five effect sizes were included in the analysis (mixing subjective and objective attentional measures) this finding was largely exploratory.
<!-- text p.1 -->

According to ART, 'bottom up', involuntary attentional processes (Corbetta & Shulman, 2002; Fan et al., 2005) are less dependent on directed attention, and thus task performance may be largely unaffected by directed attention fatigue. Conversely, higher order tasks that depend on (top-down) directed attention will be differentially impacted by attentional fatigue, meaning these tasks have more potential for between-group (nature vs non-nature) restorative differences to emerge than tasks where performance is driven by bottom-up processes (Berman et al., 2008).
<!-- text p.2 -->

Ohly et al. (2016) performed a meta-analysis of the results of 31 ART studies using objective cognitive outcomes. They pooled the effects of environmental interventions on performance independently for different standardised cognitive tests, enabling investigation of whether performance on certain tasks is more sensitive to restoration in nature than others. While nature had a positive effect on Digit Span For­ ward/Backward Test (DSF/B) and the Trail Making Test Part B (TMTB) performance, non-natural control settings had a positive effect on the Search and Memory Task. Performance from all other tasks did not differ between environmental settings.
<!-- text p.2 -->

Stevenson et al. (2018) built on Ohly et al.'s (2016) approach by grouping cognitive outcomes from 46 studies into different 'cognitive domains', based on similar task characteristics or underlying attentio­ nal/cognitive processes the task purported to measure. For example, where Ohly et al. distinguished effects for the Necker Cube Pattern Control Test (NCPCT) and conflict scores on the Attention Networks Test (ANT), Stevenson et al. pooled the effects from these tasks in a domain labelled 'Attentional Control'. In total, Stevenson et al. identified seven cognitive domains including Attentional Control, Cognitive Flexibility, Impulse Control, Processing Speed, Vigilance, Visual Attention, and Working Memory. Small-to-moderate standardised mean differences (SMD) favouring natural settings were found for Working Memory, Cognitive Flexibility and Attentional Control. In further subgroup ana­ lyses, actual nature was associated with larger restorative effects than virtual nature exposures, and effects in some domains were larger when there was greater restoration potential (i.e., an initial fatigued status).
<!-- text p.2 -->

More recent meta-analyses of singular task outcomes support the notion that working memory (DSB performance) improves with nature exposures (Stenfors et al., 2019), though nature benefits are less clear on executive network outcomes of the Attention Networks Test (Johnson et al., 2021). While these findings align with those made by Stevenson et al. (2018), no statistical contrast has been conducted to quantify the presence/absence of nature restoration benefits for different cognitive domains. Rather, differences in statistical significance between inde­ pendent meta-analyses have been used to evidence restorative effects existing in a select few cognitive domains. This can be inappropriate given the inherent reliance on statistical power (Borenstein et al., 2021; Harrer et al., 2021) which can differ greatly across cognitive domains. For example, working memory research is prevalent in ART literature, likely resulting in comparatively highly powered meta-analyses relative to other domains (e.g., creativity). Claims that pooled SMDs meaning­ fully differ between cognitive domains can be better supported by sta­ tistically quantifying those differences through formal subgroup analysis, rather than informal comparison of results generated by in­ dependent meta-analyses of each domain.

## [INTRODUCTION] 1.2. The current review

Building and extending upon previous systematic review and metaanalysis of ART literature, this review intends to provide novel synthe­ sis and analysis of the quantitative evidence regarding the effect of natural environments on cognition. Adopting Stevenson et al.'s (2018) strategy of grouping cognitive outcomes into domains, this review uses contemporary statistical analyses to investigate previously unexplored variables and provide an updated review of the broad and burgeoning ART literature.
<!-- text p.2 -->

The primary aim of the current review was to investigate exposure duration as a potential moderator. The duration of environmental ex­ posures in studies ranges widely, from short 'micro' exposures lasting under a minute (Johnson et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2015), to 'moderate' length exposures under an hour (e.g., Amicone et al., 2018; Berman et al., 2008; Wade et al., 2020), to longer exposures of several hours (Triguero-Mas et al., 2017). In prior meta-analyses, the effects associ­ ated with these varying intervention durations have been pooled together without further exploration. Given dose may be a crucial factor for intervention efficacy, empirically clarifying the relationship between exposure duration and effect size is essential, particularly for the development of evidence-based guidelines and practice recommenda­ tions for nature exposure treatments.
<!-- text p.2 -->

In this review, we operationalise duration of exposure (minutes) as the 'dose' of acute environmental interventions, and speculate that restorative effects in each environmental setting are likely to follow a dose-response curve - though presumably with differing rates of response (faster in nature; e.g., Fig. 1a). Such a relationship would indeed predict the previously observed pooled effect sizes favouring nature (e.g., Stenfors et al., 2019; Stevenson et al., 2018), though based on the restorative logic of ART (i.e., restoring a resource to baseline) these differences cannot be maintained linearly over extended exposure durations. That is, nature cannot perpetually improve cognition to a greater degree than non-natural settings when the end result is ho­ meostasis, which will also eventually be reached under any conditions where directed attention is suitably disengaged (Kaplan, 1995). Instead, we predict a non-linear relationship between exposure durations and between-group SMDs should emerge (approximating the shape in Fig. 1b), which is tested in the current review via quadratic meta-regression. However, it is possible that nature benefits not strictly due to directed attention restoration to a baseline level, but involve enhancement of some other interrelated function to improve cognition (i.e., 'instorative' effects; Gidlow et al., 2016; Hartig, 2007). Compelling linear relationships between exposure duration and effect size could plausibly support instorative perspectives.
<!-- text p.2 -->

The current review also differs from prior research in analytic strategy regarding pre-post intervention data to assess restoration. Pre­ vious meta-analyses all make an effort to consider pre-intervention performance, whether by basic post/pre subtraction of SMDs (Bowler et al., 2010), or categorising studies as 'baseline balanced' (SMD <0.1 at pre-test) and then implementing sensitivity (Ohly et al., 2016), or by subgroup analyses (Stevenson et al., 2018). Despite these methods, each publication still makes inferences about performance change based on the analysis of post-test data only. Furthermore, these strategies involve removing valid, randomised, pre-post experimental data from analyses. To retain maximal data from the literature while ensuring outcomes are not biased by pre-intervention differences, we analyse pre-to-post-intervention change scores, contrasting performance im­ provements between natural and non-natural settings. This shifts the analysis to metrics that reflect the effect of the intervention, with a null hypothesis that each environmental intervention produces an equal change in performance.
<!-- text p.2 -->

Lastly, there is substantial methodological heterogeneity in the ART literature. Interventions can be either 'real' (actual) or virtual, with each of these able to be either sedentary or active. If active, the degree of activity can range from gentle walking to vigorous exercise. For virtual exposures, the mode and immersion of the exposure varies, including still images only, audio only, images and audio, video only, video with audio, and so on. On top of these variations, primary studies are commonly not only interested in a general environmental effect, and include other experimental manipulations to investigate interactions. These can be physical adaptations to the exposures, such as completing them with friends, phones, or laptops present. Alternatively, manipu­ lations can target relevant non-physical constructs, such as social iden­ tity (Morten et al., 2017). Such factors likely moderate environmental effects, as previously seen with actual vs virtual exposures (Stevenson et al., 2018). Therefore, the current review will investigate moderating factors via subgroup analysis to determine if these methodological dif­ ferences in experimental design are associated with differences in pooled effect sizes.

## [INTRODUCTION] 1.3. Objectives and hypotheses

Objective 1: Estimate the effect of nature interventions on preversus-post cognitive performance change, relative to that of nonnatural interventions, for each distinct cognitive domain. In addition, statistically test the difference between within-domain effect sizes to add validity to conclusions regarding presence/absence of effects in given domains. The pooled SMD was predicted to be larger in cognitive do­ mains which theoretically rely more heavily on directed attention (H1), such as Working Memory, Attentional Control and Cognitive Flexibility (Stevenson et al., 2018).
<!-- text p.3 -->

Objective 2: Within each cognitive domain, estimate the predictive utility of exposure duration on the average SMD of performance improvement between natural and non-natural exposures and deter­ mine what shape best describes this dose-response relationship. A quadratic relationship between exposure duration and SMD was hypothesised.
<!-- text p.3 -->

Objective 3: Within each cognitive domain, exploratory subgroup analyses will estimate the impact of methodological sources of hetero­ geneity in predicting the average SMD between natural and non-natural exposures.
<!-- text p.3 -->

## [METHODS] 2. Materials and method

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and Cochrane recommendations (Higgins et al., 2023). The review protocol was registered with PROSPERO in August 2022 (registration ID: CRD42022352330).
<!-- text p.3 -->

## [METHODS] 2.1. Literature search & selection process

A systematic search strategy was devised by the research team with the assistance of independent research librarians from the University of Tasmania. The goal of the search was to capture any experiments examining changes in objective cognitive performance following expo­ sure to natural environments or stimuli. Three concepts were used to systematically search the literature: nature, cognition, and tests/tasks. These three concepts mirror the search strategy employed previously by Stevenson et al. (2018).
<!-- text p.3 -->

The final list of search terms (supplementary material S1) was applied to search the titles, abstracts and keywords of articles in MED­ LINE, PsycINFO, SCOPUS, Web of Science and EMBASE databases. The literature search is current up to August 24th, 2023. The database searches (an initial 2022 search and 2023 update) were downloaded to Endnote21 (Clarivate, 2013) and screened for duplicates, before being uploaded to the online software Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, 2023) for screening. Additional relevant studies identified in the refer­ ences of screened articles were manually included if appropriate. Title/abstracts of all search results, and subsequent eligible full texts, were each independently double-screened by the review team. CB (corresponding author) screened all titles/abstracts and full texts, with second screening undertaken by one of the other authors. Any dis­ agreements between reviewers were explored and resolved during res­ olution meetings. Details of disagreements are not reported nor statistically quantified, in accordance with Cochrane guidelines (Higgins et al., 2023).

## [METHODS] 2.2.1. Population

All study populations were eligible, regardless of age or clinical condition.
<!-- text p.4 -->

## [METHODS] 2.2.2. Intervention & comparator

Included studies implemented a mixed within-between experimental design, where an experimental and comparator group were measured pre-and-post intervention. Allocation to experimental/comparator groups must have been randomised (individual or cluster). Experimental group interventions must have included the presentation of 'natural' stimuli, whether fully immersive or instances of nature added to otherwise control settings. Comparator group interventions must have been (predominantly) void of 'natural' stimuli, either as passive control groups or as alternative exposure groups to urban/built stimuli.
<!-- text p.4 -->

Given a focus of the review was to assess the influence of exposure duration, only results from one-off acute interventions were synthesised, rather than cumulative longitudinal programs. This distinction was not applied during screening, with ineligible longitudinal designs identified during data extraction.
<!-- text p.4 -->

## [METHODS] 2.2.3. Outcomes

Included studies needed to report pre- and post-test data from at least one objective, behavioural measure of cognitive performance.
<!-- text p.4 -->

## [METHODS] 2.3. Data extraction

A standardised data extraction spreadsheet was developed by the research team, based upon the Cochrane (Higgins et al., 2023) and Covidence templates. Study authors were contacted where information was unclear or unattainable from the publication. Data extraction was carried out by CB. Pre- and post-test means (M) and standard deviations (SD) were extracted on all normally distributed cognitive outcome measures. Crucially, change-M and change-SD (required to generate SMDs of performance change following interventions) were extracted or calculated. Where change-SDs were not reported, unable to be obtained via author follow-up, or unable to be calculated via summary statistics, these values were imputed using the recommended methods outlined in the Cochrane Handbook (Higgins et al., 2023). The source of Change-SD values (True vs Imputed) was included in subgroup analyses.
<!-- text p.4 -->

## [METHODS] 2.4. Quality appraisal & risk of bias

The quality appraisal (QA) system was an adaptation of a fieldspecific system designed and used by Ohly et al. (2016) and Stevenson et al. (2018). It combines key indicators and items from three inde­ pendent review tools to provide a context-appropriate assessment of evidence in its degree of robustness and potential for bias. The QA checklist in the current review comprises 19 items, scored as 'yes' (2), 'partial' (1), 'no' (0) or 'unclear' (0), with total scores out of 38 expressed as a percentage. When scoring of QA items in primary studies were unclear, authors were contacted directly for clarification. See supplementary material S2 for quality appraisal checklists for eligible studies.
<!-- text p.4 -->

The Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB2; Sterne et al., 2019) instrument was used to identify risk of bias in randomised trials across the key domains of randomisation processes, deviations from intended in­ terventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and se­ lection of reported results. The RoB2 allows for varying bias assessments for different outcome measures within a single study, with a judgement algorithmically generated for each domain along with an overall RoB for the outcome. All QA and RoB assessments were undertaken by CB. See supplementary material S3 for the RoB profile of all included outcome measures.
<!-- text p.4 -->

## [METHODS] 2.5. Data synthesis and analysis

Outcome measures were categorised according to overarching cognitive domain as deemed by the research team. The set of cognitive domains established by Stevenson et al. (2018) was used as an initial framework and expanded upon. A detailed rationale for cognitive domain classification for each cognitive task/outcome is presented in supplementary material S4.
<!-- text p.4 -->

Correlated and Hierarchical Effects (CHE) three-level meta-analyses were planned to allow for the inclusion of dependent observations and retain maximal nuance in the extracted data. This approach limits reviewer-imposed common-denominator decisions regarding data in­ clusion (Hartig & Jahncke, 2017), however, some combining of data was still required to avoid 'unit of analysis' issues (Higgins et al., 2023). Following Cochrane recommended practice, we combined intervention conditions where necessary to avoid double counting, unless this would limit the ability to run subgroup analyses related to comparator condi­ tions (urban vs control) or type of exposure (actual vs virtual), in which case the n of each condition was manually divided by the number of times that condition appears.
<!-- text p.4 -->

Cluster randomised data were integrated using effective sample size adjustments via design effects, following Cochrane guidelines, using an assumed IntraCluster Correlation (ICC) value of 0.03. ICC values of <0.05 are typical and increase as cluster sizes decrease (Higgins et al., 2019). We included sensitivity analyses where cluster randomised n were adjusted with varying ICC values between 0 and 0.1 (supplementary material S5).
<!-- text p.4 -->

Once all contrasts were finalised, SMD effect sizes, corrected for small samples (Hedge's g), were calculated using the Change-M and Change-SD of each natural vs non-natural contrast identified in the eligible studies. All outcomes were coded such that a positive value indicated an improvement on the outcome. Positive between-group SMD values indicated an effect favouring nature interventions, with inter­ pretation guided by Cohen's (2013) guidelines (0.20 = small, 0.50 = moderate, 0.80 = large). All meta-analyses were undertaken using the metafor package in R (Viechtbauer, 2010).
<!-- text p.4 -->

2.5.1. Objective 1 - pre-post intervention effects within cognitive domains SMDs were pooled within each cognitive domain using independent CHE three-level meta-analyses with Robust Variance Estimation (RVE). The constant sampling variation was assumed to be 0.6. Sensitivity an­ alyses for varying correlation values are plotted in supplementary ma­ terial S6. CHE-RVE multilevel models not only account for the nonindependence between multiple effects from a single study, but explic­ itly model a correlation structure of effect sizes within each study and protect against model misspecification and heteroscedasticity in coefficient estimates (Harrer et al., 2021; Pustejovsky & Tipton, 2022). The significance of effects in each domain cannot be validly contrasted to each other, as these domain analyses essentially represent observa­ tional subgroup analyses (Higgins et al., 2023). Formal, statistical contrast of within-domain effects occurs via subgroup analysis treating each domain as a subgroup within the broader dataset of cognitive outcomes.
<!-- text p.4 -->

A Subgroup Correlated Effects (SCE) RVE three-level model, with an assumed constant sampling correlation of 0.6, was undertaken to sta­ tistically test the hypothesis that average SMDs differ between the identified cognitive domains. SCE models are akin to CHE models within subgroups, but treat nested effects independently between subgroups (Pustejovsky & Tipton, 2022). This allows for the statistical comparison of subgroup effect estimations exactly as they were estimated indepen­ dently. In essence, after fitting each independent cognitive domain CHE meta-analysis, the SCE model can quantify the difference between those pooled SMDs.
<!-- text p.5 -->

## [METHODS] 2.5.2. Objective 2 - moderating effect of exposure duration

Meta-regressions were undertaken by adding exposure duration as a covariate to each within-domain CHE-RVE model. Linear and quadratic terms of Exposure Duration were included allowing model fit compari­ son (Akaike Information Criterion; AIC) of linear and non-linear re­ lationships between exposure duration and between-group SMDs. Following Cochrane recommendations (Higgins et al., 2023), meta-regressions were only undertaken in domains with 10+ observations.
<!-- text p.5 -->

## [METHODS] 2.5.3. Objective 3 - subgroup analyses of hypothesised points of heterogeneity

Lastly, individual subgroup variables are added to each domain's unmoderated model to assess for differences in estimated effects. SMDs incorporating true change statistics were first contrasted against SMDs based on imputed change-SDs, to determine if the imputation method biased the estimates. The following subgroups were then contrasted via independent inclusion in the model: Type of nature exposure (Actual/ Virtual), mode of nature exposure (Visual/Auditory/Immersive), Den­ sity of nature exposure (Nature scene/Control scene with added nature), Type of comparator (Urban/Control), Activity (Exercise/Walking/ Sedentary), Experimental fatigue induction (Yes/No), Other concurrent manipulation (Yes/No), Single Cognitive task (Single/Battery), sample age demographic (Children/Teens/Adults/Older-adults), clinical sam­ ple (Yes/No). Following Cochrane recommendations (Higgins et al., 2023), subgroup analyses were only undertaken in domains with 10+ observations. If a subgroup had <2 observations restricting pooling of effects, the subgroup was merged to retain the data in the analysis (e.g., Walking and Exercise exposures combined as an 'Active' subgroup).
<!-- text p.5 -->

## [METHODS] 2.5.4. Heterogeneity estimates

Heterogeneity is reported for each fitted CHE model via two statis­ tics. I 2 is reported in accordance with Cochrane conventions (Higgins et al., 2023) to depict the proportion of overall heterogeneity attribut­ able to variance in true effects, at both within-study (Level 2) and between-study (Level 3) levels. However, given I 2 does not portray the absolute heterogeneity of effects (Borenstein, 2019, 2024), we primarily convey heterogeneity using 95 % prediction intervals (PI) which depict an estimated dispersion window for true effects, relative to the pooled mean.
<!-- text p.5 -->

## [METHODS] 2.5.5. Multiplicity correction

Given the large number of significance tests, a study-wide correction for multiplicity was applied, with effects deemed statistically significant at an alpha of 0.01 (Borenstein et al., 2021; Polanin & Pigott, 2015; van Agteren et al., 2021), thus reducing the type I error rate to 1/100 tests across the entire publication.
<!-- text p.5 -->

## [METHODS] 2.5.6. Publication bias

Risk of small-sample bias (consistent with publication bias) was assessed through visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger's Regres­ sion Tests adapted for dependent data (i.e., Egger multilevel models; Rodgers & Pustejovsky, 2021). Where problematic funnel plot asym­ metry and/or significant Egger's tests were observed, exploratory cor­ rections were applied by deletion of outlier effects. In addition, cluster bootstrapped selection models (metaselection package; Pustejovsky et al., 2025) provide selective-reporting-corrected model parameter es­ timates, a novel method for assessing and handling publication bias-like reporting with dependent data. Results from these sensitivity analyses are reported in supplementary material S7.
<!-- text p.5 -->

## [RESULTS] 3.1.1. Search results & study characteristics

A total of 7056 records were identified from database and citation searching. Following screening, 86 articles, containing 93 individual studies/experiments, were deemed eligible for data extraction (see Fig. 2 for PRISMA flow diagram). These 93 studies included approximately 1 6363 participants (61% women) across a range of child (<18 years old; n = 912), adult (≥18 years old n = 5288) and older adult samples (as defined by primary study authors; n = 163). Ten studies recruited from 'clinical' populations (n = 130 psychological conditions; n = 377 physical conditions). Mean sample age ranged from 6.8 to 86. Most studies recruited relatively young, university student participants (51%), with the median mean sample age being 22.1 years. A total of 23 countries contributed to this literature (Supplementary material S8a) with the earliest publication year being 1991 (Hartig et al., 1991), while 46 studies (~50%) were published in the previous five years from 2019 to August 2023 (Supplementary material S8b).
<!-- text p.5 -->

As outlined in section 2.2, four studies with longitudinal programs were excluded from the quantitative synthesis. A further nine studies were excluded during data extraction, primarily due to inability to obtain a relevant or normally distributed M, SD, or n (Fig. 2). Data from 80 studies (74 publications) were thus included in the final metaanalyses (Table 2). Of these, 50 (63%) were parallel trials, while 30 (37%) studies were crossover trials with washout periods ranging from 2-min breaks to multiple weeks. Participants in six studies were cluster randomised based on classroom/lecture membership (Amicone et al., 2018; Lin et al., 2014; Morton et al., 2017; Wade et al., 2020) or experiment date availability (Johnson et al., 2019; Schertz et al., 2022). Studies employed either Virtual (56%) or Actual (i.e., 'real'; 43%) environmental exposures. Most study interventions were sedentary (59%). Active-exposure studies included 11 with exercise, 17 with walking, and one design incorporating walking and exercise. Three studies did not specify an exposure activity but were unlikely to be sedentary (e.g., school recess free play). Nature exposures mostly consisted of full nature scenes (89%) through actual immersions or virtual presentation of the scene. Nine studies involved otherwise Urban/Built or control settings that had some degree of nature added to it; greenery added to urban streets or city rooftops (Jiang et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2015; Lin et al., 2014), pot plants in office spaces (Evensen et al., 2015; Ma & Shu, 2018; Raanaas et al., 2011), a house with relative openness to the nature outside (Tanaka et al., 2013), and nature sounds played in an indoor setting (Ma & Shu, 2018; Van Hedger et al., 2019). Comparator settings were mostly Urban/built scenes (66%) as opposed to control scenes. Overall, there were 86 comparator settings within the 80 studies, with 6 studies having nature groups contrasted against both urban and control settings (Emfield & Neider, 2014; Jenkin et al., 2018; Ma & Shu, 2018; Neilson et al., 2021; Rogerson & Barton, 2015).
<!-- text p.10 -->

In 20 studies, additional manipulations occurred alongside the environmental interventions. These included instructions regarding breathing or relaxation techniques (Blum et al., 2019; Sahlin et al., 2016), mindfulness practice (Lymeus et al., 2017) and imagery scripts (Chow & Lau, 2015), instructions to ruminate (Berman et al., 2012) or count to 1000 (Chen et al., 2020) during exposures, the presence of other friends, strangers, phones or laptops during exposures (Greenwood & Gatersleben, 2016; Jiang et al., 2019; Johansson et al., 2011; Rogerson et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2016), interactive or perturbated exposures (Crossan & Salmoni, 2021; Manchon & Simunic, 2023), awe vs mundane setting exposures (Collado & Manrique, 2020), intermediate cognitive assessments during exposures (Hartig et al., 2003), varied amounts or colour of nature (Lin et al., 2014; Zhang, Jin, et al., 2023), inanimate objects present in control exposures (Evensen et al., 2015) and the manipulation of salient social identity (Morton et al., 2017) or cognitive strategy (Bourrier et al., 2018). Some of these were applied to all par­ ticipants, while others applied only to single conditions.
<!-- text p.10 -->

Regarding fatigue and cognitive demand, 58 studies administered a single cognitive test, while 22 studies administered two or more tests at pre/post-test. The order of these task batteries was sometimes counterbalanced, randomised, or matched. Experimental fatigue in­ duction occurred in 23 studies (29%). Two of these were betweensubjects manipulations (Hartig et al., 2003; Wang et al., 2018). Hartig et al. placed a long cognitive fatiguing phase after pre-testing. Although not all studies have cognitive testing immediately pre/post intervention, this method marked a qualitative deviation from other pre-to-post change scores in the analyses. As such, that specific contrast from the loading task arm of Hartig et al. (2003) was omitted from meta-analysis.
<!-- text p.10 -->

Acute exposure durations ranged from 40-s (Lee et al., 2015) to 60-min (e.g., Schertz et al., 2022). Triguero-Mas et al. (2017) included a longer exposure, but their design involved a lengthy measurement phase after a 30-min exposure, before an additional 3-h exposure. We opted to include the initial 30-min exposure to avoid risk of carryover effects and double inclusion of data. Generally, a lack of information regarding the randomisation process and pre-registered analysis plans generated concerns in most studies (see supplementary material S3). Overall, we downgraded most algorithm-generated risk of bias scores by one cate­ gory based on the apparent experimental rigour and cognisance of publication-era norms.

We note that not all items reflect study quality in all contexts, particularly for studies not explicitly researching attention restoration. Indeed, given it is unclear that designs involving cognitive fatigue are of higher quality than those without, the 'demon­ strated need for restoration' item may be better suited as a descriptive subgrouping variable than a quality indicator. In general, study quality scoring was lowered by a lack of reporting a priori power calculations and randomisation processes, and over representation of nongeneralisable student samples.

## [DISCUSSION] 4. Discussion

This review synthesised the current scientific evidence from rando­ mised, pre-post intervention experiments that detail the difference in restorative effects between natural and non-natural environments, and the influence of exposure durations on these effects. All average effect sizes favoured nature conditions, though only reliably for Working Memory and Attentional Control. Despite some variation in the pooled effect size magnitude across cognitive domains, these differences were not statistically significant. A consequent combining of outcomes from all cognitive domains together saw the effect significantly favour nature. Exposure duration moderated this effect size in a non-linear relation­ ship, which also varied within specific domains.
<!-- text p.15 -->

## [DISCUSSION] 4.1.1. Observable, but non-significant, effect size magnitude differences between cognitive domains

The pooled effect estimates for all 11 cognitive domains favoured nature conditions, with sizes ranging from negligible to small (omitting Creativity). Working Memory had the largest effect, followed by Logic/ Critical Thinking and Attentional Control. In contrast, effects were negligible for Processing Speed, Basic Attention Span, Vigilance/Sus­ tained Attention, Orienting, Cognitive Flexibility, Visual tracking, and Inhibition. Regardless of magnitude, the four most observed domains (Attentional Control, Vigilance/Sustained Attention, Working Memory and Inhibition) predictably emerged with the most reliable effect esti­ mates, with confidence intervals less than 0.16 standard deviations from their pooled effect. Of these, only the Working Memory and Attentional Control effects showed reliable favouring of nature conditions, with natural settings being significantly more restorative than non-natural comparators for Working Memory.
<!-- text p.15 -->

Consistent with H1, informally comparing domain effect sizes tended to depict larger effects favouring nature in domains that encompass higher order cognitive functions, while the weaker effects occurred in domains that are less demanding or associated with bottom-up processing. This aligns with ART and observations from previous metaanalysis (Stevenson et al., 2018), however, informal comparisons of effect size magnitude have limited capacity to formally evidence the presence/absence of effects in different cognitive domains (Borenstein et al., 2021).
<!-- text p.15 -->

A formal statistical contrast of all cognitive domain effects contra­ dicted H1, whereby no effect sizes significantly differed from one another. Despite Working Memory having the largest (and only statis­ tically significant) effect, this difference in effect size from the other cognitive domains was not statistically meaningful. Crucially, this sug­ gests that quantitative differences of this size could occur even when no true qualitative difference exists between how nature exposures influ­ ence restoration in these domains. We thus cannot conclude with cer­ tainty that nature restores working memory any differently than it does any other cognitive domain - a more conservative interpretation relative to using only within-domain statistical significance to evidence the presence/absence of effects across domains (e.g., Stevenson et al., 2018).
<!-- text p.16 -->

However, an additional six years of ART literature have now been synthesised to produce a highly aligned finding to Stevenson et al. (2018). In particular, the contrast of two heavily researched cognitive domains in the ART literature, Working Memory and Vigi­ lance/Sustained Attention, which are also generally representative of the top-down/bottom-up distinction of direction attention reliance and need for restoration (Ohly et al., 2016). As with Stevenson et al., we observed a significant Working Memory effect and no such effect for Vigilance/Sustained Attention. This is a quasi-replication not to be ignored. We can be confident the within-domain differences in our analysis are not merely attributable to the number of observations in each domain and resulting statistical power, as the Working Memory and Vigilance/Sustained Attention domains were virtually equally rep­ resented. Taken together (along with other Working Memory meta-analysis findings; Stenfors et al., 2019), we tentatively suggest that a nature restoration effect does exist and is larger for working memory, in comparison to vigilance/sustained attention outcomes where the presence of an effect is less clear. However, we stress again that this conclusion is descriptive only, not based on inferential statistics gener­ ated by the SCE analysis. It appears the effect sizes themselves, and thus the differences between them, were too small to statistically distinguish in our formal comparison.
<!-- text p.16 -->

## [DISCUSSION] 4.1.2. Relationship between effect size and exposure duration

When all cognitive outcomes were analysed together, exposure duration significantly moderated effect size. Consistent with H2, this effect was non-linear, such that there was an increasing nature benefit associated with increasing exposure durations - up to a point. Specif­ ically, it appears the average nature benefit peaks around 30 min of exposure, before benefits may begin to subside - although effect esti­ mates are less reliable at longer durations.
<!-- text p.16 -->

Within-domains, this relationship is seen again for Working Memory, Attentional Control and Inhibition, although of these, only Working Memory effects were significantly moderated by exposure duration. Cognitive Flexibility effects were also moderated by exposure duration, but in the opposite direction to H2. We do not expect that Cognitive Flexibility has a truly opposite relationship with exposure duration compared to other domains and cognitive outcomes overall, but may be influenced by a highly weighted study with a large effect size favouring nature after a long (1-h) exposure. This highlights the observational nature of meta-regression and potential for spurious relationships to emerge which are unrelated to the moderator in question.
<!-- text p.16 -->

Several within-domain analyses predicted a linear relationship with exposure duration to be a better fit to the data than non-linear (Visual Tracking, Orienting and Sustained Attention/Vigilance). This contra­ dicts H2, and the restorative logic of ART more broadly. However, none of these linear effects were highly reliable estimates, and none contained effect sizes from exposures above 30-40 min. As such, these domains may be reflecting only the first half of the non-linear relationship described above. Overall, a non-linear relationship between exposure duration and effect size is more supported than a linear relationship.
<!-- text p.16 -->

## [DISCUSSION] 4.1.3. Subgroup analyses & heterogeneity of effects

Only fatigue induction emerged from subgroup analyses as a factor influencing effect size magnitude with any consistency. Including fa­ tigue induction produced the best model fit to the overall data, where the nature restoration effect was larger for fatigued samples. Nature benefits were also larger for fatigued samples within each domain, though these differences were only reliably greater for Inhibition and Working Memory. These findings align with theorised effects where domains with greater recruitment of directed attention will be more susceptible to fatigue and thus nature restoration. Unfortunately, there were no fatigued-samples in the Vigilance/Sustained Attention domain to allow for a contrast in more bottom-up cognitive domain. This reflects a surprising lack of data regarding a key premise of ART (cognitive fa­ tigue) in one of the more highly researched ART outcomes.
<!-- text p.16 -->

The absence of other reliable subgroup effects was surprising, as these variables were expected to explain some degree of heterogeneity of effects - which was substantial across and within domains. Although average effects reliably favoured natural environments overall, as well as for Working Memory and Attentional Control domains specifically, the heterogeneity indicated a wide dispersion of effects, such that the true effect could plausibly favour either natural or non-natural settings. This remained so when exposure duration significantly moderated effect size. Exploratory analyses removing outliers reduced heterogeneity consid­ erably, however, there was no basis for removal of these data beyond statistical non-conformity. Instead, these are likely valid experimental observations of what appears to be a highly heterogenous effect.
<!-- text p.16 -->

Interestingly, the prevailing tendency was for true effect variance to be occurring within-studies. This may explain the limited evidence of heterogeneity when comparing our subgroups, as they are primarily between-study manipulations. Aside from sampling variance that makes up approximately one third of overall heterogeneity, it appears differ­ ences within individual studies may be most pertinent to effect hetero­ geneity. Experiments regularly include several different cognitive tasks/ outcomes that would fall in the same meta-analysis (e.g., Bratman et al., 2015; Johnson et al., 2022). This could lead to differences in interven­ tion effects across metrics (e.g., raw totals, difference scores, response times, accuracy percentages and error rates), particularly where scores themselves can be correlated (e.g., speed/accuracy trade-offs; Bogacz et al., 2010).
<!-- text p.16 -->

Ohly et al. (2016) analysed effects on specific outcomes and observed non-significant heterogeneity, although, with so few observations available once analysing individual outcome measures, heterogeneity estimates are far less reliable (Borenstein, 2019, 2024). Future meta-analyses with even more data may opt to increase outcome spec­ ificity to quantify how dependent variable differences impact hetero­ geneity of nature restoration effects.
<!-- text p.16 -->

## [DISCUSSION] 4.2. Heterogeneity in the ART context

A broader issue is how the substantial heterogeneity of effects mat­ ters to the interpretation of our results. Generally, meta-analysing a collection of effects is justified when they are 'sufficiently homogenous'; by synthesising evidence on apples and oranges one can void making a meaningful finding for either (Higgins et al., 2023). However, this analogy is only true if you believe the effects of each fruit (population) are of interest independently, otherwise, this combination can provide meaningful information about fruit in general (Higgins et al., 2023).
<!-- text p.16 -->

Some reviews, such as those on drug efficacy, must explore sub­ stantial heterogeneity because it indicates the drug may be harming some populations (if the PIs cross zero), they need to find out who these populations are and ensure they are not recommended the drug. In our context, outcomes are strictly positive (restoration), with values below zero merely indicating the benefit may be larger for a non-natural exposure in some populations. While we strive to fully explain effect heterogeneity in the same way, 'nature' is an extremely heterogeneous intervention, and heterogeneity of its effects may be an unsurprising byproduct. It is also not certain that determining the specific combina­ tion of niche individual/intervention factors that characterise the het­ erogeneity would translate to improved outcomes for the general population.
<!-- text p.16 -->

Our pooled effects can be highly generalisable and informative by depicting the expected average benefit of nature exposures, even with the caveat that effects could vary broadly from person-to-person in the real world. We currently have minimal evidence to explain this het­ erogeneity, after considerable exploration of straightforward population subgroup differences (e.g., adult vs children, actual vs virtual exposures, exercise vs sedentary, etc.). It is therefore plausible that heterogeneity is instead driven by a nuanced aggregate of these subgroup combinations, which may be further obscured by individual difference variables and variation in measurement techniques. In sum, dissecting the 'apples from oranges' in search of effect homogeneity may negatively impact the generalisability of research findings, where average effects of nature are quite reliable and have broader utility.
<!-- text p.17 -->

## [DISCUSSION] 4.3. Strengths, limitations and future research

Strengths of this review include screening for high quality, rando­ mised pre/post experimental outcomes from objective measures; ana­ lyses that appropriately handle hierarchical data structures and correlation of effects while maximising data inclusion; an expanded range of moderator analyses; performance change-statistics that accu­ rately represent 'intervention effects'; and valid statistical comparisons focusing on effect estimates, confidence intervals and scaled heteroge­ neity estimates (PIs). However, the search, screening, and analysis strategy are not without limitations.
<!-- text p.17 -->

As with any systematic search strategy, it is possible for primary study titles/abstracts to use alternate or unforeseen wording which can result in otherwise relevant studies failing to appear in the search re­ sults. Our decision to omit the word "nature" as a standalone term in the search (and consequent use of proximity operators to link it to with other related terms from the literature) may have increased such a risk. This decision was due to an untenably excessive amount of unrelated mate­ rial retrieved during the pilot search, driven by the word's polysemous nature. To mitigate the risk associated with this choice, we manually screened any citations with apparent face-validity for inclusion in our review that were referenced in articles that received full-text screening. A further limitation was the possibility that there were excluded articles from grey literature and non-English journals/databases, that may have otherwise met all remaining eligibility criteria targeting experimental rigour. Non-English articles were excluded due to an inability to validly translate such texts, and grey literature was excluded with the aim of enhancing study quality through peer-review. However, future review efforts could apply expanded search strategies and eligibility to avoid the limitations of these decisions.
<!-- text p.17 -->

Additionally, by applying a common denominator design there can substantial nuance lost in comparison to the analyses conducted in primary studies. This is mostly seen in the loss of interactions and the influence of covariates with our natural/non-natural contrasts either collapsing across or separating these additional variables altogether. For example, where Collado and Manrique's (2020) analysis allows for interaction between environement (Natural/Urban) and Awe (Awe/­ Mundane), our extraction of their data created two natural-vs-built ef­ fects, one for the awe conditions and another for the mundane conditions. We also omitted effects that reflected pre-to-post change from multi-day or weeks-long interventions. While opting against pooling effects from such clearly distinct modes of intervention (relative to the predominating minutes-long exposures) improved the specificity and reliability of the effect estimates, this decision precluded further investigation of other time-related variables, such as frequency of exposure, and how this may interact with each exposure duration. Along with other design elements, such as the categorisation of cognitive do­ mains or intervention characteristics into subgroups, we acknowledge this form of review-author decision making represents an inherent limitation to evidence synthesis.
<!-- text p.17 -->

In hypothesising the effect of exposure duration, we frame inter­ vention duration as a proxy of 'dose' and predicted a dose-response curve (section 1.2). However, this operationalisation may carry an inherent limitation. As previously outlined by Bratman et al. (2019), in medicine/toxicology (where these terms are predominantly applied) there is a distinction between the amount of a substance that merely reaches the organism (i.e., exposure) and the amount of a substance actually absorbed by the organism (i.e., the dose). It is likely that a similar distinction occurs for environmental interventions; even where exposure durations are matched, experimental samples could vary in their engagement with the environment and thus true 'dose'. Experi­ ments included in this review addressed these concepts (e.g., differing engagement due to presence of devices; Jiang et al., 2019). It is also likely that previous experiences in nature influence one's current experience (e.g., Conditioned Restoration; Egner et al., 2020). Overall, the engagement with, and thus experience of these experimental nature interventions is a largely unknown variable, and likely interacts with exposure duration to influence restoration. This factor was unable to be accounted for in the current review.
<!-- text p.17 -->

The ability to draw meaningful conclusions from our subgroup an­ alyses within-domains is limited by reducing statistical power with fewer studies/effects, and the inherent observational (non-causal) na­ ture of subgroup analysis. On several occasions, the identical grouping of effects/studies would constitute two different sub-group comparisons; for example, all virtual exposures in the Orienting domain were seden­ tary, while all actual exposures involved walking, creating a perfect overlap (and thus identical analysis results) for the two different sub­ group contrasts. Had a meaningful relationship emerged, it would be impossible to determine which intervention heterogeneity was more relevant - indeed, it is also possible that an unknown factor could be responsible for any observed subgroup correlation.
<!-- text p.17 -->

Future research can increasingly focus on mixed methods to provide greater insight to the qualitative experience of nature interventions, alongside the observation of quantitative restorative effects. Our illus­ tration of the evidence (Fig. 3) provides some insight to lacking exper­ imental observations, such as in clinical and older adult samples - particularly in longer exposure durations - or outcomes measuring vigilance/sustained attention after fatigue induction. Future experi­ ments can address such gaps. Future reviews can synthesise longitudinal studies to expand investigation of how 'amount of exposure' influences effects, including a potential cumulative effect of exposure frequency within such programs. Lastly, a systematic evidence map (e.g., White et al., 2020) could be an ideal method to formally address the vast and heterogenous evidence base regarding nature exposures and various health outcomes outside the scope of the current review.
<!-- text p.17 -->

## [DISCUSSION] 4.4. Conclusion

In summary, the current review sought to 1) estimate the benefit of natural vs non-natural exposures on cognitive performance for different cognitive domains, 2) assess the relationship between nature benefits and exposure durations, and 3) investigate potential sources of hetero­ geneity. Our findings indicate that on average, nature interventions are reliably more cognitively restorative than non-natural ones. This effect is also reliably observed in Working Memory and Attentional Control domains, although no differences in domain effects were themselves statistically significant. The average nature benefits are likely to be even more pronounced when the samples underwent a form of experimental fatigue induction prior to the intervention. Furthermore, the overall cognitive difference is significantly moderated by exposure duration, whereby nature benefits tend to be greater over short breaks, with a less clear distinction between natural and non-natural exposures when du­ rations near an hour, potentially due to a homeostatic ceiling effect. However, this same relationship was not observed in each cognitive domain, with effects again greatest for Working Memory and Attentional Control.
<!-- text p.17 -->

We conclude that for cognitive performance improvements following acute (<1 h) breaks, nature exposures provide on average a greater benefit for directed attention restoration, particularly from states of cognitive fatigue. These benefits appear maximal after approximately 30-min of exposure, making nature exposures appear particularly suited for maximising short breaks from cognitively demanding tasks - a common circumstance in virtually all modern educational and occupa­ tional contexts. We believe these findings may guide any future devel­ opment of recommendations in practice guidelines for the provision of clinical and/or non-clinical interventions to improve health outcomes relating to cognitive restoration.
